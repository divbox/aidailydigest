Meta returns to open weights with Muse Glimmer
Latent Space

Meta released Muse Glimmer, a 30B dense multimodal agent model under Apache 2.0, alongside Mark Zuckerberg's follow-up to last year's "Personal Superintelligence" essay. It's Meta's fourth open release in this cycle following the Dreamer acquisition, Muse Spark, and Muse Code — but the first one paired with a full renewed manifesto, and Alexandr Wang has confirmed Muse Spark 1.2's weights are coming "soon."

Technically, Glimmer looks deliberately built for local deployment rather than benchmark chasing. Community analysis points to Gemma-4-style hybrid attention with scale-free QK norm, deeper vision encoding, and longer sliding-window attention — and, notably, the model was logit-distilled from Muse Spark and trained from scratch on agentic traces rather than following the conventional base-then-post-train pipeline. Meta pairs it with a DFlash speculative decoding drafter and 4-bit quantization that brings the LM under 20GB, aiming for fluid interaction on consumer hardware.

Artificial Analysis places Glimmer at 35 on its Intelligence Index — just behind Qwen3.6-27B (38) and around Kimi K2.5 (36) — with a standout 44 on their Openness Index. Weaknesses show up in hallucination and knowledge calibration, and it trails peers on general agentic knowledge work, though it holds up on tool-use benchmarks like Tau3-Banking. The headline pitch is single-node self-hosting: ~60GB in BF16, ~18GB at 4-bit, 128K context.

The release lands inside Zuckerberg's broader argument that concentrated frontier AI power favors institutions over individuals, and that Meta's bet is on personal agents, entrepreneurship tools, and free-or-affordable access as the counterweight. He also leaned into the geopolitical framing directly, arguing that "any policy that slows American model releases — even by a month — could add significant risk to American leadership" against China's buildout pace. Reaction from open-source figures like Andrew Ng and Clement Delangue ("Meta is back") suggests the community is reading this as a genuine re-commitment, not a one-off.

Claude pushes Riemann Hypothesis bound from 41.6% to 67.2%
Latent Space

An unreleased Anthropic research variant of Claude was set loose on the Riemann Hypothesis — the 160-year-old conjecture that all nontrivial zeros of the zeta function lie on a single critical line. It didn't prove the full hypothesis, but it improved the best known lower bound on the proportion of zeros provably on that line from 41.6% to 67.2%, the largest single jump in the problem's history.

The scale of the search is the real story: Claude coordinated 60 subagents running in parallel inside Claude Code, tried 650 failed approaches, and executed roughly 2,400 shell commands over a day and a half — reportedly spanning 31M output tokens of repeated retries and large-scale exploration. The breakthrough came from treating the zeros as one unified space rather than analyzing them individually — a reframing, not a new theorem.

The result was verified formally in Lean 4 and reviewed by two external mathematicians before Anthropic published the proof code and process logs publicly, even though the model itself remains unreleased. Mathematicians reacting online framed it less as "RH progress" in the traditional sense and more as a striking demonstration of AI-assisted theorem search operating at a scale and persistence no human team could sustain.

It's part of a pattern worth watching: both this result and OpenAI's GPT-5.6-Cyber zero-day finds came from unreleased models, suggesting the most interesting frontier capability signals right now are showing up before anything ships — in research previews and internal red-teaming rather than product launches.
