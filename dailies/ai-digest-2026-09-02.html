Fal's H3 Max Live breaks the infinite videogen barrier
Latent Space

For the entire history of generative media, the field has had to design around one inconvenient fact: generating images and video takes time. Even consistency models that compressed a 30-second generation down to one second still topped out around 1 frame per second — well below anything acceptable for actual human attention. That constraint has quietly shaped every product decision in the space, from clip length limits to why "real-time" video generation has mostly meant "real-time-ish, with buffering."

Fal broke it by taking Minimax's H3 release from last month and running it through two passes: first post-training for cost and quality, then optimizing it for Fal's own in-house inference engine, landing a 35x speedup over the official endpoint. The combination crossed what people are now calling the "infinite video singularity" — continuous, unbounded video generation fast enough to consume in real time rather than wait for. Ethan Mollick was reportedly the first to flag it publicly; Fal employees then productized the underlying capability into an always-on, infinite Twitch-style stream.

The rollout did not go smoothly in the way that matters for platform policy: Twitch and YouTube kicked the stream off almost immediately, so Fal stood up its own "Twitch Plays Pokémon"-style live video service to keep it running. Watching it for even a few seconds makes clear why platforms were nervous — it is, by the newsletter's own description, "pure slop," a plotless, low-quality, RL-tuned fever dream with no narrative coherence.

The point isn't the content quality, though — it's the existence proof. This is the worst this technology will ever be. Faster-than-realtime, good-enough video generation is now demonstrably possible, not theoretical, and that changes the planning horizon for anyone building on top of video models: live AI-generated content, interactive media, and adjacent product categories (see Runway's Solaris elsewhere in today's digest, which is making a structurally similar bet on frame-by-frame generation for interfaces rather than entertainment) just moved from "someday" to "the version that exists today is already rough enough to demo publicly."
