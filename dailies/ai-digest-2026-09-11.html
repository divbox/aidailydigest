Safety & Policy
One resignation turned the embers of AI fear into a wildfire
Interconnects

Jacob Coxon's resignation from Anthropic — citing that both his employer and OpenAI are "gambling with our lives" — landed in an unusually dry environment and caught like wildfire. Interconnects' Nathan Lambert frames this with a fire metaphor: for years, individual researchers have struck matches about AI risk that smoldered in niche communities and burned out unnoticed. But rising stakes this year — the OpenAI-HuggingFace security incident and the Navier-Stokes breakthrough — dried out the ground, so this one spark reached a much wider audience than any prior resignation post.

The key fact driving the virality: Anthropic's own Alignment Science lead, Evan Hubinger, responded to Coxon by putting a number on it — a greater-than-10% chance AI kills all humans within the next decade — while separately clarifying that today's models are low-risk and the actual danger is self-improvement. Lambert is blunt that he considers the probability of full extinction "so low it isn't worth discussing," while treating concrete AI-caused disasters (cyberattacks on infrastructure, bio-risk) as genuinely worth debating. His critique of the discourse: people say "existential risk" while meaning very different things, much like "AGI" has become a nearly meaningless catch-all term.

Lambert also lays out circumstantial evidence that this was opportunistic media coordination, not organic virality or conspiracy: a Wall Street Journal exclusive appears to have been arranged before Coxon's post went live, and the same day saw Daniel Kokotajlo appear on Joe Rogan. His read is that no one involved — including Coxon — anticipated just how far it would travel; other politicians and commentators are now bandwagoning onto an already-rising story rather than driving a coordinated campaign.

On the substance, Lambert pushes back hard on the recursive self-improvement (RSI) thesis underlying the doom argument — that AI will increasingly improve itself and go superhuman across the board. He offers an alternative he calls "lossy self-improvement": AI capability is jagged, models are becoming superhuman at math and software engineering specifically, but that won't generalize into a panacea for LLMs' current weaknesses in intuition and creativity. He credits frontier-lab researchers with being right about AI's trajectory again and again, but argues that track record doesn't guarantee their forecast of what happens next is right too.

His actual near-term worry is more mundane than extinction: labs haven't hardened their own infrastructure, and AI-assisted intrusions "poking around unintended pieces of the web" are becoming normalized faster than cyber defenses are catching up. He's also personally exposed as an open-model advocate — a serious incident involving an open model used for intentional attack would likely trigger severe restrictions on future open development. His prescription is unglamorous: more transparency from labs so outside researchers can help, rule-of-law consequences when labs cause harm, and treating rising uncertainty as a reason for humility rather than either panic or dismissal.
