OpenAI's Navier-Stokes Result, the Credit Dispute, and the Rise of Test-Time Compute at Scale
Latent Space

OpenAI says an internal model "significantly more capable than GPT-6 Astra" produced a proposed proof of the Navier-Stokes existence-and-smoothness problem — one of math's seven $1M Millennium Prize problems — using roughly 10,000 agents running in parallel for 88 hours, followed by another 17 hours of Lean formalization and verification. Outside estimates put the run at around 130B output tokens and $10M–$40M in API-equivalent compute cost. The technical headline isn't really the math: it's that unstructured, massively parallel test-time compute plus orchestration is now a first-class scaling axis, alongside pretraining and post-training — and several observers pointed to the same cost-collapse trajectory that took ARC-AGI from hundreds of thousands of dollars to tens of dollars per run.

The result arrived wrapped in a priority dispute. NYU's Tristan Buckmaster and Anthropic's Levent Alpöge say they had spent roughly a year pursuing the same proof strategy, feeding drafts into Codex and posting partial results the night before OpenAI's announcement. Sam Altman and Sébastien Bubeck's account is that OpenAI heard rumors the pair had solved a related Euler problem, tested whether its own models could replicate the harder Navier-Stokes case, and then offered coordination and possible lead authorship once it realized the overlap. OpenAI has stated it accessed no specific user data for the effort, while conceding it can't rule out that de-identified derivative data improved its models more generally over time.

The more interesting reaction came from mathematicians, not AI researchers. Terence Tao's cautionary comments — amplified by François Chollet and Gary Marcus — framed the real risk: if a mere rumor of progress can trigger an industrial-scale AI effort that "flattens" a research direction overnight, open fields have an incentive to go quiet and stop sharing work in progress. That's a bigger long-run cost to science than who gets the citation on this particular proof.

The same news cycle nearly buried two other stories that would normally have been the lead: Meta's launch of Muse, a consumer personal-agent product built on isolated per-user VMs with a separate "Sentinel" process mediating every sensitive action (payments, app access, approvals) rather than exposing secrets directly to the agent — plus a public bug bounty up to $300K. Meta says day-one usage beat internal projections by 10x. Paired with OpenAI's result, it's a snapshot of the same week agentic infrastructure (sandboxing, oversight, large-scale parallel compute) matured on two completely different fronts at once.

When Will Average People Feel AI's Impact?
Interconnects

Nathan Lambert's core argument: AI optimists keep reaching for industrial-revolution comparisons, but that framing skips a crucial difference in how the change actually reaches people. The First and Second Industrial Revolutions delivered immediately tangible goods — cheaper clothing, indoor plumbing, sewing machines, better light — that ordinary people could touch and use within years. AI's current touch-points for most people are, by contrast, "fringe, marginally beneficial, or confusing": image generation, a somewhat better search engine, and a string of headlines (like OpenAI's Navier-Stokes claim the same week) that mean almost nothing to someone outside the field.

Even the optimistic long-run case — new scientific discoveries, therapeutics for rare disease, sustained economic abundance — routes through channels too indirect for people to credit AI specifically. Lambert's example: if AI-assisted drug discovery eventually produces a miracle cure, most patients will credit their doctor, not OpenAI or Anthropic. He estimates it's plausible that the average American's home, car, and daily routine look largely unchanged even 50 years out, even as the technology underneath compounds enormously — self-driving being a partial, and mostly independent, exception.

The political risk this creates is the piece's sharper point. Knowledge work — roughly half the U.S. economy — is already being reshaped by AI "as fundamentally as electricity," but the other half of society isn't along for the ride, and Lambert doesn't expect the tech industry to grow headcount even as output explodes. He cites "Engels' pause" — the 1790–1840 period when British wages stagnated while GDP expanded rapidly during industrialization — as the closest historical analogue, and warns that if AI's boom looks like that to people not benefiting from it, the backlash is justified, not irrational.

His close is a warning wrapped in a bet: AI's two live problems are that its benefits are currently too indirect, and that it inherited Big Tech's political baggage largely by bad timing rather than its own actions. Solving either would buy the industry more patience. If robotics and self-driving eventually fuse with the current LLM wave, he thinks that's the path to something people can actually feel — ironic, since much of the AI discourse has spent years insisting LLM progress is a fundamentally different story from older robotics/AI efforts.
