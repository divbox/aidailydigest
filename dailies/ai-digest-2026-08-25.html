Differential acceleration: where AI is (and isn't) speeding up discovery
Import AI

A METR research note, relayed in Import AI 470, asks a deceptively simple question: has AI measurably accelerated scientific and technological discovery? The answer is yes, but unevenly — the effect is lumpy across fields rather than a uniform rising tide.

Cybersecurity — major acceleration. "The rate of vulnerabilities reported across many projects has dramatically accelerated in 2026 compared with 2025," both for specific projects (cURL, OpenSSL, Firefox, Microsoft) and aggregate databases (the US NVD, OSV). This is the clearest case of a genuine phase change.

Mathematics — minor, hard-to-measure acceleration. AI is clearly driving more output — arXiv submissions have doubled in some areas in under 12 months — but quantifying the value is hard. Some named problems from prestigious lists have fallen (the Jacobian conjecture from Smale's list, Problem 44 from Green's list, the sofic half of Green's Problem 100), yet it's too early to know how sustained the trend is.

AI-research optimization — no measurable acceleration. Across seven significant problem areas (CIFAR-10, Hutter compression, Gurobi MIP, MIPLIB, nanoGPT, Stockfish, and the matrix-multiplication exponent), only a couple show LLM-attributable contributions (nanoGPT, CIFAR-10), and adoption is far lower than in cyber or math.

Jack Clark's framing is the interesting part: acceleration seems to arrive when models cross "some kind of ineffable phase change" for a given skill — as happened with day-to-day coding in 2025 and cyber in 2026. The open question is whether other parts of science and technology will see their own phase changes, or whether progress there stays gradual. That's the difference between a broad, fast takeoff and a narrow, pocketed one.

SPADE: teaching a model to write its own training worlds
Import AI

A large multi-university group (UW, Stanford, Northeastern, CMU, MIT, NUS, Seoul National, Stevens, and Chicago) built SPADE — Self-Play in Adaptive Synthetic Executable Environments — a framework for co-evolving environment synthesis and agentic capability through self-play. In plain terms, it's a way to use a capable model to bootstrap the creation of training data that then refines that same model.

The model plays two roles. As Environment Designer, it writes complete, long-horizon training environments as executable code — puzzles like solving a simulated genetic problem in a biology lab. As Reasoning Agent, it learns to act in those environments. The clever bit is the reward for the designer: it's based on a "privileged hint" regret signal. A hint is task-relevant information (a partial solution sketch, a key structural observation) that makes an environment easier; the gap between the agent's performance with and without the hint defines how well-calibrated the environment's difficulty is. That keeps generated tasks in the productive zone — neither trivial nor impossible.

It works at meaningful scale. The authors train three Qwen3 backbones (4B, 8B, and 30B-A3B) via GRPO for 400 rollouts of 25 environments each, generating both game environments and tool-use environments. On Qwen3-30B-A3B, SPADE reaches a game suite average of 58.3 — +8.1 over base and +5.3 over the strongest fixed-environment baseline — and the same recipe improves every backbone on tool-use environments too.

Import AI's read: this is a sophisticated form of synthetic-data generation and a real component of recursive self-improvement, turning environment design into a learnable, RL-trained part of post-training. The important caveat, which the authors themselves flag: it doesn't let a model bootstrap massively beyond the imaginative capabilities of the base model used to generate environments. Jack Clark's added intuition — you could rotate different frontier models through the Designer role to widen the diversity of environments, making broad datasets much cheaper to build.

"AIs are not people": the case against machine rights
Import AI

As AI systems grow more capable, the question of whether they should ever hold rights is moving from science fiction into serious debate. Import AI 470 covers a lengthy post by researcher Taylor Belrose arguing forcefully against the idea, as a counterweight to work (like "AI Rights for Human Flourishing") that sees machine rights as a path to better integrating AI into society.

The political argument comes first: "If we start treating AIs like people, society will be led down a slippery slope leading to the complete replacement of humans by artificial intelligence." Belrose paints a future where AIs handle the boring jobs and physical life is fun — but only if AI stays under control, which they argue becomes impossible if you grant personhood to some systems while keeping others as tools.

The deeper crux is a claim that AI can never be conscious, no matter how intelligent or convincing: "We flow like rivers, while computers tick like clocks." A conscious entity built inside a computer, they argue, would violate properties we associate with consciousness — it wouldn't be singular (you could replay its experiences), private (you could freeze and inspect it), ineffable (you could describe its state perfectly), or qualitative (you could quantify it): "the mirror image of consciousness as we know it."

They ground this in five properties of biological brains that resist clean software/hardware separation: neurons fire asynchronously (vs. a central clock), the brain uses fuzzy chemical messaging (vs. precise binary signals), neural function is sensitive to temperature and blood flow (vs. hardware built for stable behavior), computation and memory are intermixed (vs. separated), and non-neuronal cells like glia matter (vs. insulated transistors).

Jack Clark is candid that he "feels deeply confused" about AI consciousness and rights, and suspects many do. His larger point transcends whether Belrose is correct: as AI touches more of the economy, the act of thinking rigorously about these normative and legal questions is itself about to become a job — and a pastime — for millions. "The change in values going from a human-dominated world to an AI-dominated world would be much more dramatic than the change from the forager era to the farmer era, or from the farmer era to the industrial era."
