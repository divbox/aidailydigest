Safety & Policy
Much ado about Open Weights
Latent Space

Latent Space's framing for the day is that everyone is writing a lot, but only Kimi K3 actually shipped — and that gap between discourse volume and artifact volume is the story. The week's policy fight has been almost entirely rhetorical, conducted through open letters and position posts, while the thing the letters are arguing about landed from a lab that signed nothing.

The sequence matters. On July 24 Jensen Huang used his first X post to put "Open Weights and American AI Leadership" in front of Washington with 25 signatories; within a day it hit 50, absorbing OpenAI, Google, AMD, Cisco, Cloudflare, GitHub, Block and Ollama alongside the original bloc of Meta, Microsoft, Hugging Face, Mistral, Mozilla, IBM, Cohere and the Linux Foundation. The ask is narrow — don't impose premature restrictions on downloadable model weights — but the coalition is unusually broad, spanning chipmakers, hyperscalers, open-source foundations and VC.

Amazon and Anthropic stayed off every version, and David Sacks made Anthropic's absence a talking point. Anthropic answered on July 27: Amodei's statement that the company "has never advocated for a ban on open-weights models," that non-dangerous open models are a public good, and that the real levers should be chip export controls, anti-distillation enforcement, and mandatory pre-deployment safety testing applied symmetrically to open and closed models.

That's a coherent position, and it's also not the letter's position. The letter is about restraint on restrictions; Anthropic's counter is about redirecting restriction to different targets — compute and testing rather than distribution. Declining to sign while insisting you were never against open weights is a fine distinction to hold in public, and the fact that the signature is still missing is more informative than the post explaining why the missing signature was misread.

The subtext throughout is China. Washington is weighing restrictions on Chinese model availability specifically, which makes "should open weights be restricted" and "should Chinese open weights be restricted" two questions the debate keeps collapsing into one. Kimi K3 shipping mid-argument is a fairly direct answer to whether restriction-by-policy can outrun release-by-upload.

Models & Research
Kimi K3: the largest open-weight release to date, and the license underneath it
Latent Space

Kimi K3 is a 2.8-trillion-parameter mixture-of-experts model with a 1M-token context window, released as full weights on July 27 after an initial API launch on July 16. It's explicitly agent-shaped — the pitch is coding and long-horizon knowledge work rather than chat — and it takes the top spot on the Frontend Code Arena, reportedly above Claude Fable 5. Day-0 hosting across inference providers meant it was usable the moment it was downloadable.

The practical constraint is size. At 2.8T total parameters this is not a model anyone runs casually; the community's attention has gone straight to MXFP4 quantization as the path to making it deployable on anything short of a serious cluster. "Open weights" and "runnable weights" are diverging as frontier open releases scale, and K3 is the sharpest example so far — the artifact is public, the capability to serve it is not evenly distributed.

The license is the part that got read late. Kimi K2, K2.6 and K2.7 Code shipped under what everyone called Modified MIT; K3's Hugging Face repo carries a custom document instead. Two clauses do the work. Clause 2 requires anyone operating a Model-as-a-Service business to sign a separate commercial agreement with Moonshot once revenue across the licensee and its affiliates crosses $20M over any consecutive 12 months. Clause 3 requires prominent "Kimi K3" attribution in the UI for products above 100M MAU or $20M monthly revenue.

As Kevin Xu put it, the $20M MaaS threshold is comically low — it captures essentially every hyperscaler, neocloud and inference platform that would plausibly host K3. Internal enterprise use is carved out: if you're running K3 for employee tooling and not exposing it, its outputs, or its capabilities to third parties, clauses 2 and 3 don't bite. That carve-out is what keeps it defensibly "open" for the largest population of users.

Read together, this is a well-constructed piece of go-to-market rather than an act of pure release. Moonshot gets the distribution, mindshare and benchmark placement of an open frontier model, and simultaneously builds a revenue-share obligation into every commercial reseller. It's a strategy that only works if the model is genuinely good enough that platforms want to host it — which, on the Frontend Code Arena result, it appears to be.
