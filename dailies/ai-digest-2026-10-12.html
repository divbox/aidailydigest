Multi-agent orchestration: lots of fan-out, mixed payoff
Latent Space

Claude Managed Agents dynamic workflows (public beta, multiagent_20261001) let a lead agent write a phased plan, fan it out to up to 1,000 agents per run, and merge the results. Anthropic advises starting with scoped tasks because token use can be high. Claude Code Projects also opened to all waitlisted Pro/Max users, with each project running tasks as parallel threads and sessions now able to run locally.

The cautionary data point comes from Vals AI on Vibe Code Bench. Running GPT-6 Sol and Opus 5.5 as teams cost 1.8–5.1x more, and only Sol at medium effort improved significantly (+7.3 points). The behaviors differed: Sol delegated in parallel along architectural lines, while Opus ran sequential waves of about 6.8 subagents and about 1,140 subagent tool calls per app at max effort, with no significant gain.

On the other side, Prime Intellect had 2,000+ agents rewrite Prime Agent in Rust over two weeks, using 10K+ sandboxes and 200B+ GLM-5.3 tokens. The result reaches usable input about 13x faster with 83% less startup memory. An accompanying essay argues that context limits make swarms inevitable. Devin now spawns trees of managed Devins, so wall time tracks the slowest branch rather than the sum.

Codex added a Windows sandbox on Microsoft Execution Containers and Pro-only next-message predictions, but also drew outage complaints. DHH says GPT-6.1 Sol made Codex his primary tool over Claude. Takeaway: orchestration primitives are shipping faster than evidence that fan-out pays for itself, and the wins so far come from big, decomposable, verifiable jobs like rewrites.

Models & Research
I expect rapid progress but not towards general superintelligence
Interconnects

Nathan Lambert starts with a puzzle: top industry researchers keep telling him AI will be better than them at their jobs within a few years, and he couldn't articulate why he doubted it. His answer is that researchers are watching a massive acceleration in infra and engineering capability. He agrees models will be superhuman distributed GPU engineers in a few years, which will make experimentation and tinkering far easier, but it won't make the models dramatically different in nature.

He frames this as a swing in the field's research/engineering balance. Before deep learning, AI was mostly research; today top researchers are judged by their ability to implement and scale ideas in complex infrastructure. As agents absorb the engineering, the bottleneck shifts back toward ideas, in line with Ilya's "era of research" claim. Some call this RSI; Lambert prefers the more grounded "parallelized, AI-assisted language modeling."

The mechanism is verifiability. Training throughput (tokens/sec/GPU) and inference cost (FLOPs per token, cost per answer) are highly optimizable, with established sub-problems. He expects agents to push inference close to hardware limits and the effective cost of intelligence to fall near-exponentially, possibly faster than recent trends. Next comes longer-horizon accelerator/model co-design. He calls automated pretraining research in architecture and data selection within 2–3 years reasonable.

Downstream, he predicts a Jevons-paradox demand surge for agentic models, since the industry is bottlenecked on delivering agents more than on frontier capability (Meta's Muse is his example). In science, models' strength at crawling literature and connecting sparse sub-fields could either herald a new era of discovery or just speed up the arc science was already on.

He also flags RL environment quality as industrial-scale low-hanging fruit. RL data companies are crossing $100M–$1B in revenue, yet researchers widely call the average output "crap". Labs still see clear ROI, and he argues the problems are fixable. (The emailed version is truncated at this point; the full post continues on the web.)

Decision models become a category — and TypeSafe/Jev owns the reference point
Latent Space

Latent Space's lead covers TypeSafe's "Series AI", at a reported $7.5B valuation, and Sequoia's "leak" that Jev crossed $100M ARR in its first week. Jev's API has been cloned everywhere, and the issue argues that only one company gets to create a category. Astroturfing accusations are acknowledged, not resolved.

The cloning was on full display in a single day. OpenAI's Decisions API (predicate / choice / score, text+image, GPT-6 Luna, $0.10/M input, free output), Microsoft-Decision-1 (aimed at LLM judges and hypothesis screening), Perplexity pplx-decider-v1.1-27b (claims 94.5% on Decision Bench at $0.017 per 1K decisions), Cloudflare clef (clef-omni takes audio/video/image/text; clef-flash undercuts Jev on price; weights on HF), and Liquid d1 on Vercel AI Gateway.

The infrastructure followed just as fast. vLLM Semantic Router's Decision 2.0 answers several questions about one input in a single pass. LangSmith uses Jev as a judge that returns typed difficulty and correctness labels per trace. Unsloth published a notebook that turns Qwen3.5-4B into a decision model on 8GB of VRAM. One walkthrough took Qwen3.5-0.8B from 37% to 65% accuracy in 60 steps.

Why harnesses care: many agent steps are classification, not generation. LangChain says routing each task to the cheapest adequate model cut median Open SWE cost per task by 64%. On the research side, Apple/CMU's Selection-based Structured Reasoning scores six natural-language strategies by length-normalized log-likelihood in one batched, KV-shared pass. Per-turn reasoning latency drops more than 90%, but end-to-end only 28–54%, at roughly baseline success (61.37% vs 61.25%). An early evaluator notes decision models still struggle with consistency on complex calls.
