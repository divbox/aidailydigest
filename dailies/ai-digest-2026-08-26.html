Business & Industry
Andrew Ng gets into AI Engineering
Latent Space

Andrew Ng — Google Brain and Coursera cofounder — has relaunched DeepLearning.AI with an explicit focus on AI Engineering, a milestone Latent Space (which coined "Rise of the AI Engineer" back in 2023) treats as a validation moment. The framing isn't vibes: it's grounded in an analysis of over 10,000 job postings, dozens of structured interviews with hiring managers and recruiters, and survey data.

Ng distills the discipline into four skills. Building and deploying AI applications is the one closest to the traditional MLE/MLOps loop — understanding LLMs, context engineering, RAG, and agentic workflows, plus the statistical discipline to measure, steer, and govern systems through evals and error analysis. Software engineering fundamentals is the classic SWE track: knowing which tradeoffs even exist so you make better stack, architecture, and data-store decisions — and can give a coding agent the right context instead of letting it silently make poor choices.

The third, using coding agents, is the skill that was least evident when the "1000x AI Engineer" idea first surfaced in 2023 with Copilot as the only game in town. It exploded across 2024–2026 with Cursor's 0-to-$60B run and the rise of Claude Code, Codex, and Cognition. It's about having a mental model for how agents work, knowing when to intervene versus leave them alone, working with (and sometimes without) a clear spec, orchestrating multiple agents, and continually re-learning as best practices churn. The fourth, shaping the build, is the one the original essay didn't foresee — product sense and business context, knowing when to ship an MVP versus slow down and build carefully.

Latent Space's editorial read: "AI Engineering Skills" are now broadly applicable well beyond people with the literal job title, and the lines between AI Engineer, AI PM, and Design Engineer keep blurring in both directions. That blur — not any single tool — is the real story of how the role is generalizing across the industry.

The harness is the new optimization surface: persistent agents, rollback, and enterprise MCP
Latent Space

A clear theme ran through this issue: agent quality is increasingly shaped by the harness, not just the base model. NVIDIA's new evaluation work argues that structural checks on agent "skills" barely predict usefulness — scan scores correlate with judged quality at just Spearman ρ = 0.14 — and proposes measuring "Skill Lift" instead: run the same task with and without a skill under identical conditions and score the delta in completed work. A companion position paper claims enterprises should standardize on a single reusable coding-agent harness rather than bespoke orchestration graphs, arguing harness choice can matter more than model choice on real enterprise work.

Persistent, self-modifying agents are moving from concept to open source. Andy Konwinski introduced Headlong, a "microharness" for agents that think continuously rather than only on request — storing trajectories as a DAG of jsonl files and running a self-guided inner loop; it reportedly pulled off an unattended self-debugging repair in 48 minutes, at the cost of $1–$2/hr of background thinking and occasional self-inflicted failures. Complementing it, exo is a recursive self-improvement architecture with an append-only event log, swappable executor, and snapshot/rollback-capable sandbox — explicitly designed so agents can rewrite their own prompts, tools, and memory without being able to corrupt durable state. The common thread is durability, forking, and rollback, not better prompting.

That maturity extends to plumbing. Anthropic's enterprise-managed auth for MCP connectors centralizes authorization through an org's identity provider, removing per-tool OAuth for connectors like Asana, Atlassian, Notion, and Slack — closing a real gap between toy demos and auditable deployment. The MCP roadmap adds long-running workloads with streaming/server push, HTTP for local servers, progressive discovery for large catalogs, and standard delegated identities.

The reddit recaps drove the point home concretely: the same Qwen3.8-27B model failed a C#/OpenGL ocean-rendering task under a VS Code Copilot setup but succeeded under an agentic harness with execution and screenshot feedback — even generating a PNG decoder on the fly. As the community put it, sandboxed tooling can badly under-represent what a coding agent can actually do. Model plus harness is becoming one unit, and evaluating a model in isolation is losing meaning.

Cost-normalized benchmarks and token-accounting hygiene reshape model choice
Latent Space

The most decision-relevant trend in this issue was cost-normalized agent benchmarking. Together AI reported that under a fixed $100 budget, GLM-5.3 completed roughly 5x more work than Fable 5 on DeepSWE — about 17 solved tasks versus 3 — despite similar first-try performance. In the same vein, GPT-5.6 Sol Max hit 72.7% on DeepSWE v1.1 at $6.47/task against Fable 5 Max's 69.7% at $21.63/task. When the spread on cost-per-solved-task is 3–5x, headline accuracy numbers stop being the thing that drives model selection.

That reframing puts pressure on how token usage is even reported. One DeepSWE run showed 918.9M input tokens — most of them cache hits — prompting the blunt argument that counting cached input tokens in "token usage" is misleading. On the eval side, practitioners warned that when a quantized model exceeds its reference on a benchmark, it may signal overfitting the quant rather than genuine improvement, with the broader lesson that fixing the eval often matters more than hill-climbing it.

Serving-side work rhymes with this. Speculative Programmatic Tool Calling (sPTC) overlaps tool execution with generation for a modest 1.0–1.2x gain but shifts optimization toward workflow pipelining. vLLM's AgentX 1.0 results on real multi-turn coding traces emphasize KV offload, prefix reuse, and prefill/decode disaggregation as the real levers for agentic throughput — not classic single-turn serving metrics. And on-device, Liquid AI's Pipette exposed a phone-scale Pareto frontier where sub-1B-active MoE models beat "smarter" reasoning models that simply don't fit mobile memory and latency budgets.

Put together, the picture is an ecosystem that's growing up around efficiency-per-dollar and measurement integrity — cheaper models doing more real work per budget, and increasing skepticism toward benchmarks and token counts that flatter the model rather than the user.
