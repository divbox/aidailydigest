Import AI 477: Jack Clark tells the IMF the singularity has begun
Import AI

Import AI 477 is the text of the Per Jacobsson lecture Jack Clark gave at the IMF meeting in Bangkok on October 17. His thesis: 2026 brought convincing evidence that AI is starting to do frontier science, sometimes autonomously, and that the singularity Vernor Vinge described in 1993 has begun. He says he'll expand on the stakes and risks in coming issues.

The evidence is a mix of examples and metrics. AI systems have proposed solutions to open maths problems (Navier-Stokes, the Jacobian Conjecture, several Erdos problems), designed viruses and drug candidates now in trials, and operated lab equipment remotely. Humanity's Last Exam went from 8% in January 2025 to 67.7% for Claude Opus 5.5, and FrontierMath from 2% to 94%.

The sharpest anecdote is TheoPhysEval: 50 unpublished theoretical-physics problems written by physicists. Models scored in the high eighties, but GPT-6-Astra insisted the expert answers were wrong, and on review the experts found subtle errors in their own calculations. Corrected, Astra reached 98% (49/50). Clark frames this as the teacher becoming the student, and as a test of creativity, the last capability needed to automate AI research itself.

On economics, he argues the key change is cheap, instantly copyable researchers: in place of roughly 10 million human scientists, imagine a billion virtual ones supervised by humans. Anthropic's own modelling suggests US GDP could reach $44 trillion by 2030 under fast-diffusion assumptions. The prescription is to build the next layer of infrastructure (robotic labs, scientific equipment, a faster path from discovery to clinic and factory) the way compute and power were built.

The second half is about restraint. Clark calls for strict human oversight of AI-driven biology, deliberate decisions about how much oversight AI-driven AI research needs ('otherwise we could lose control'), likely new rules, and choices about how much of AI-made science humans need to understand. He closes asking for 'unusual and serious rules' for the technology and the companies building it, and pacing its development.
