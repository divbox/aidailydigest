Safety & Policy
AI designs 16 working viruses from scratch, and the biosecurity rules don't exist yet
Stanford and Arc Institute researchers used genome-language models Evo 1 and Evo 2 — trained on DNA the way LLMs are trained on text — to generate full genetic blueprints for 16 new bacteriophages, published in Science on August 7. The viruses only infect bacteria and target drug-resistant infections, but a companion editorial from Johns Hopkins biosecurity researchers bluntly states the governance to safely steer this capability doesn't exist yet. Separate teams have already shown fine-tuning on excluded data can recover dangerous capabilities, including predicting how a virus evades the human immune system — this is a live dual-use problem, not a hypothetical one.
Rundown AI
Mistral open-sources Shieldstral, a 3B moderation model you reprogram in plain English
Shieldstral is a 3B-parameter open-weights safety classifier that takes your moderation policy as plain-language text at inference time instead of training against a fixed set of harm categories, reducing every moderation call to a tunable yes/no probability. It runs on a single 16GB GPU, covers 12 languages, and reportedly matches guard models 7x its size on text safety while setting a new state of the art on multimodal moderation. The pitch: change your policy without retraining, which most guard models can't do.
Alpha Signal
SecureForge auto-rewrites coding-assistant system prompts to cut security flaws
Researchers used a genetic algorithm (GEPA) to iteratively evolve a coding assistant's system prompt — running Semgrep against the generated code to catch vulnerabilities, then having GPT-5.4 propose a fix, repeating until gains plateau. Across the LLMs tested, the optimized prompts cut security flaws to 11.8% on average without hurting code quality. It only defends against vulnerability classes it was tuned against, so treat it as raising the floor, not closing the gap on novel exploits.

OpenAI, Amazon, Microsoft, Cursor, and Vercel ship Agent Plugins 1.0
Five companies published an open, vendor-neutral packaging format that bundles Agent Skills and MCP servers into one portable folder any AI client can read — before this, building a tool for agents meant a separate package for Claude, Cursor, Copilot, and ChatGPT each. Google joined as a core maintainer on launch day and shipped two implementations (Agents CLI, Data Agent Kit) rather than just an endorsement. It's currently a "Working Draft" covering packaging only — marketplace, permissions, and discovery are explicitly left to each client, so real adoption is still the open question.

Business & Industry
AMD acquires Taalas, betting on chips that hard-wire a single model into silicon
Latent Space

AMD announced a definitive agreement on August 6 to acquire Taalas, a Toronto-based startup founded in 2023 that builds AI inference chips unlike anything else on the market: instead of streaming a model's weights from high-bandwidth memory on every forward pass — the design every GPU shares — Taalas etches the weights permanently into the chip's transistors. Its first test chip, the HC1, serves Meta's Llama 3.1 8B at a claimed ~17,000 tokens per second per user, at roughly a tenth the power draw of an Nvidia H200. The design flow customizes about 2 of the chip's ~100 metal layers per model and can tape out a model-specific chip at TSMC in around two months.

The acquisition lands squarely in what Latent Space's AINews has been calling the "inference inflection" — the shift, argued by voices from Jensen Huang to Sam Altman, from AI-as-training-problem to AI-as-inference-problem, where token volume, not model novelty, is now the dominant cost driver. It's AMD's third AI acquisition in nine months, following MK1 in November and memory-optimization startup Mext in June, and it echoes Nvidia's own $20B Groq deal in December — both are general-purpose GPU makers hedging that a meaningful slice of inference workload moves to hardware built for one model instead of every model.

The catch is the one every custom-ASIC pitch runs into: baking a model into silicon means the chip is obsolete the moment a better model ships, and frontier labs currently iterate on a cadence measured in months, not the roughly two-year window ASIC economics need to pencil out. Taalas is reportedly about 1.5 years behind the frontier on model quality as the price of that speed. Whether that gap closes as architectures standardize, or inference chips just become a commodity tier below the frontier, is the question AMD is now underwriting with a checkbook instead of a partnership.

Financial terms weren't disclosed; the deal is expected to close in Q4 2026, subject to regulatory approval. Taalas had raised $219M in venture funding since founding. Read AMD's announcement →
