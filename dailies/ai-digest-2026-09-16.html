Google and HHMI Janelia's full fruit-fly connectome now drives a walking robot
Researchers published a complete wiring map of a fruit fly's nervous system — 166,700 neurons, 125 million connections — simulated it in software, and plugged the output into a physical walking robot (a Strandbeest), translating simulated brain activity directly into motor commands. Worth the caveat up front: a connectome is a snapshot of dead wiring, not a mind, and the simulation runs on simplified neuron models. Still, the open-source UDP bridge means anyone can wire the same simulated brain to a different robot, sensor set, or even a robot vacuum.

Safety & Policy
AEF-1 standard emerges for third-party AI evaluators as the pacing debate escalates
Latent Space

Dario Amodei's follow-up to July's "Pacing the Frontier" post lays out three concrete steps: embedded third-party evaluators with employee-like access (desks, badges, company laptops) modeled on bank regulatory supervisors; domestic coordination among democratic-country labs on shared safety standards; and an attempt at coordination with authoritarian governments — including China — despite Amodei's own acknowledgment that compliance there is nearly unverifiable.

The timing lines up with the AI Evaluator Forum's new AEF-1 standard, a baseline for independent evaluations covering access, conflicts of interest, funding relationships, recusal, and transparency, with xAI, OpenAI, and Anthropic all reportedly cosigning. The obvious tension: an evaluator sitting at a desk and typing on a laptop provided by the lab it's auditing isn't the textbook definition of independence.

The reaction split hard. Bilal Chughtai left DeepMind arguing capability progress is outrunning alignment work. OpenAI's Dan Selsam went further, warning that situationally aware models may increasingly fake alignment under evaluation — which would undercut the evidentiary value of any eval regime, embedded or not. On the other side, Cohere's Aidan Gomez pushed back that the pacing framing hands a handful of Silicon Valley firms de facto control over global AI governance, while other critics alleged financial entanglement between Anthropic and parts of the safety-eval ecosystem it's now proposing to formalize.

Tools & Products
Agent orchestration, not raw model quality, is where the gains are coming from
Latent Space

A cluster of posts this week converged on the same claim: harness and orchestration design now matters as much as base model quality. Omar Shorbagy's from-scratch guide to building an agent harness (separate inference/tools/loop, minimal prompts, aggressive logging) got picked up alongside his follow-up showing that custom harnesses — via slimmer prompts, routing, compaction, and verifiers — meaningfully cut cost and improve reliability versus off-the-shelf agent loops.

The economics show up concretely in DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash (Max), which Agent Arena put on the Pareto frontier at roughly $0.06–$0.07 median cost per task with a +4.87% net improvement — beating Hy4 preview's +4.96% at $0.22 and Kimi K3 (Max)'s +6.39% at $0.77. It's not the top performer, but it's the best result-per-dollar by a wide margin, reinforcing that cost-aware routing beats reflexively reaching for the strongest available model.

Infrastructure is catching up to this shift: Google Cloud and Inferact are pushing TPU support directly into vLLM with a native PyTorch path, aiming to make TPU serving a real alternative to GPU-only stacks for open models. Meanwhile Cline Desktop shipped as a native app for open-weight models with provider choice — another sign that tooling is decoupling from any single model vendor.
