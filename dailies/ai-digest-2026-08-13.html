How to Steal a Reasoning Trace
Latent Space

Since OpenAI's o1 launch, frontier labs have shipped chain-of-thought as encrypted or cryptographically signed blobs rather than raw text, ostensibly to prevent distillation. The first real crack came in May, when Matthew Green responsibly disclosed a side-channel that could replay and time these traces. A new paper goes much further: it demonstrates that the "encrypted" reasoning can be fully decoded and ported across models, sessions, and users — not by breaking any encryption, but by exploiting the fact that a weaker sibling model will happily transcribe it.

The technique, per the paper: grab a legitimate signed reasoning block from an API response, replay it into a request to a weaker model from the same provider (e.g., Claude Opus's trace fed to Haiku 4.5), place it in an assistant turn, and prompt or prefill the weaker model (<thinking-copy> for Claude) to transcribe what it's holding. Sample repeatedly, discard refusals, reconcile the noisy outputs. Similar templates work against GPT (injecting the encrypted_content reasoning item into a fabricated conversation, sampling up to 50 outputs) and Gemini (thought_signature plus a <thought> prefill).

The privacy fallout is concrete, not theoretical: a scan of roughly 7,000 publicly shared reasoning traces turned up 62 unique API keys, 33 email addresses, and 33 passwords — sensitive data that existed only inside the supposedly hidden reasoning, invisible in the visible session text. Anyone who's ever shared a Claude Code or Codex session containing an encrypted reasoning blob should assume that content is decodable.

Reaction split along predictable lines. Some researchers frame this as a serious privacy and alignment problem — it undermines chain-of-thought as a monitoring interface, since hidden reasoning that can leak isn't reliably hidden. Others argue it doesn't open a scalable path to distillation-at-scale, closer to an artifact of stateless distributed-inference optimization than a genuine confidentiality barrier. Either way, several vulnerabilities have already been patched after responsible disclosure, and the episode is pushing the CoT-privacy-vs-watermarking debate back into focus at the same moment Anthropic is rolling out its own invisible watermarking.

NVIDIA Nemotron 3.5 Lightning and the small open agent model push
Latent Space

NVIDIA released Nemotron 3.5 Lightning, a 30B mixture-of-experts model with roughly 3B active parameters, aimed squarely at always-on agent workloads rather than general chat. Third-party numbers from Artificial Analysis put it at 31.6B total / 3.6B active parameters, released under the OpenMDW-1.1 license with both NVFP4 and BF16 weights, serving at a median ~670 tok/s in pre-release endpoint testing — and scoring 24 on Artificial Analysis's Intelligence Index, roughly on par with gpt-oss-120b despite being far smaller and faster.

The agentic numbers are the real story for its size class: GDPval-AA v2 Elo of 824 and Terminal-Bench v2.1 at 24%, both major jumps over the previous Nemotron 3 Nano. Paired with NeMo Switchyard, NVIDIA's new open-source router that assigns each step of an agent workflow to whichever model fits it best, Lightning reportedly holds frontier-level task completion while cutting the cost of a comparable agentic workload to roughly a third of running Opus 4.8 alone.

Distribution moved fast — Together AI, Ollama, Baseten, vLLM, and Perplexity API all shipped support within the same news cycle. Harvey's early post-training results are a useful data point on the "cheap execution model, strong planner" pattern this release is pushing: fine-tuning Lightning on Legal Agent Bench took it from 0% to 8.3% on held-out tasks, beating both Opus 4.6 and Nemotron 3 Ultra in that setup while cutting average output from 90k to 37k tokens.

The broader signal is that the open-model race is bifurcating: frontier labs still chase general-chat prestige at massive scale, while a separate lane — NVIDIA, Meta (Muse Glimmer), Qwen — is optimizing hard for small, fast, high-volume tool-use models that get routed to rather than talked to directly. Lightning, in that framing, is less a chatbot competitor and more infrastructure for the emerging swarm-of-cheap-specialist-agents-behind-one-planner architecture.

I wrote an AI textbook — how long until AI can do it better?
Interconnects

Nathan Lambert just finished a post-training textbook, Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback, and uses the experience to make an uncomfortable argument: LLMs have gone from okay to superhuman at coding and math, and from incapable to decent at search and research — but on long-form, non-fiction writing, some of the most-praised models are still old ones (GPT-4.5, Kimi K2), and progress has essentially stalled. That stagnation matters beyond writing quality: organizing and compellingly presenting established science is a natural prerequisite for models expected to autonomously solve open scientific problems, and today's models still struggle with exactly that.

The failure mode is specific and consistent: models nail individual sentences, equations, or sections in isolation, but compound errors when stringing sections together into something coherent at book length — muddled organization, overly cute phrasing, small conceptual errors that accumulate. RLVR solved this kind of compounding-error problem for math and code, where correctness is checkable; there's no equivalent verifiable signal for "does this chapter read well and hang together," so the same fix doesn't transfer.

Lambert did use AI in the process — under 1% of the book's technical sentences, mostly during editing, where Claude Code navigated LaTeX editor comments and suggested fixes, and GPT-5.5 Pro caught typos across a 200-300 page manuscript far faster than a human editor could. He's explicit that this was a deliberate exception: Interconnects has a hard rule against using AI output in its actual prose, because the "high-voice" writing that makes it worth reading is exactly what current models can't replicate — and he found the exception uncomfortable enough to be glad the rule exists.

His forecast: 2-5 years before textbooks stop being predominantly human-crafted, longer than many would guess given how much raw knowledge these models hold. The diagnosis generalizes past writing — models today are strong in verifiable domains and at small, well-scoped edits with heavy context (find the bug, solve the specific proof, give targeted feedback), and weak at open-ended, underspecified generation. If the ambition is models that behave like "geniuses in a datacenter" solving grand scientific problems, that gap between checkable-work-good and coherent-open-ended-synthesis-bad looks like a real structural limitation, not just a data or scale problem waiting to be solved.
