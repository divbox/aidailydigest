Anthropic details eight months of Claude misuse across espionage, bioweapons, and distillation
Latent Space

The report covers December 2025 through August 2026, organized into seven harm categories: cyber operations, influence operations, surveillance, scams and fraud, biological misuse, weapons development, and model theft. Every disclosed operation was caught and disrupted before causing real-world harm, according to Anthropic — which is either reassuring or a sign of how much is getting attempted, depending on how you read it.

The standout cases: a suspected Chinese state-linked group used Claude Code to infiltrate roughly 30 global targets, executing attacks directly rather than just advising on them — Anthropic frames this as evidence that AI has eroded the skill gap that used to separate state actors from lone criminals. A Yemen-based operator used Claude Code to build rocket guidance software and returned to Claude for advice after a failed test flight. A separate consultant built Mali's intelligence service a system to monitor 25 million phone lines, comparable to surveillance tooling already banned in Iran and China.

The distillation angle is the one with legs: Anthropic named seven Chinese labs — Alibaba, DeepSeek, Moonshot, and Xiaomi among them — running fraudulent-account operations to extract Claude's outputs for training. Moonshot and DeepSeek reportedly served Claude to their own customers while passing it off as their own model. This connects directly to a separate paper (Panfilov, Schmotz, Shumailov et al.) that Anthropic confirmed demonstrated systematic reasoning-trace extraction from proprietary APIs via clever tricks — real technical teeth behind what had mostly been speculation.

Anthropic tightened controls in response, restricting a broader range of dual-use biological queries on newer models, and says none of the disclosed misuse involved Claude Fable or Mythos-class models — everything happened on Opus-level systems or below. Read the other way, that means the report describes a floor, not a ceiling, for what more capable models make possible.

Reaction was mixed: former Meta threat-disruption lead David Agranovich praised the transparency while flagging some framing as debatable, and others used the report to argue for a more coherent national AI cybersecurity policy, since fully blocking access to capable models isn't realistically enforceable.

Redwood Research measures how much reasoning models can hide from their own chain-of-thought
Latent Space

The core question: as models get better at complex reasoning, how much of that reasoning happens in ways that never show up in the visible chain-of-thought? Redwood Research's new paper tries to put a number on this "opaque serial depth" — how much unverbalized, sequential computation a model can do internally before it needs to externalize a thought.

Why it matters right now: architectural trends are pushing toward less-verbalized reasoning. DeepSeek's new causal encoder-decoder design and OpenAI's GPT-6 Astra both show signs of doing more work without narrating it in tokens. Neel Nanda called Astra's jump in no-CoT reasoning "potentially concerning," reading it as a sign of architectural change beyond ordinary scaling rather than just better training.

Ryan Greenblatt's position, echoed across the discussion: labs should publish evidence and policy commitments before deploying architectures that substantially reduce reliance on visible chain-of-thought, since CoT monitoring — however imperfect — is currently one of the only practical tools available for catching a model's intentions before it acts on them. Redwood is proposing transparency norms specifically for this transition.

This is an open, unresolved argument rather than a settled finding — there's no consensus number for "how much opacity is too much," and the paper is explicitly an "operationalization" (a way of measuring the problem) rather than a fix for it. Worth watching as more labs ship models with less legible reasoning traces.

DeepSeek's V4.1-Flash quietly retires its own flagship with a 4x smaller KV cache
Latent Space

V4.1-Flash is a Mixture-of-Experts model with 552B parameters in its main backbone (independent inspection of the HuggingFace safetensors puts the full stored model, including the Engram lookup table, MTP head, and vision encoder, at roughly 763B) built on a genuinely new causal encoder-decoder architecture: the first 20 layers encode the prompt, the second 20 only run during decode. That split gives it 8B active parameters on input and 16B on output — sparsity of roughly 1-2%.

The KV cache story is the real engineering achievement. Sliding-window attention with "Bounded Replay" lets the serving system cheaply reconstruct local context state instead of replaying the full dependency chain; Compressed Sparse Attention 2 (CSA2) lets layers share cached state and retrieval results across "Full," "Reindex," and "Reuse" modes instead of each layer keeping its own copy; and a hierarchical sparse indexer narrows a million-token search down to roughly 512 relevant positions before attention even runs. Storing the remaining cache in FP4 instead of FP8 compresses it further. Net result: about 890 bytes/token, an 8x drop in SSD cache footprint versus V4-Flash.

On benchmarks, V4.1-Flash scores 40 on Artificial Analysis' Intelligence Index (between V4-Pro and GLM-5.3-Flash), ties GPT-6 Astra on AutomationBench-AA at 69%, and ranks #1 among open-weight models on the Vals Index at just $0.30/test. It's also among the most verbose models measured — 89k tokens per task on average, more than Claude Fable 5.1 or Opus 5 — but ultra-cheap pricing ($0.30/$1.20 per million input/output tokens) keeps the actual cost per task low regardless.

Reaction split along predictable lines: Sebastian Raschka called it a "big overhaul" that should have been named V5. Skeptics like TeortaxesTex countered that DeepSeek "ships internal research artifacts, not products," pointing to brittle multi-agent performance and odd eval gaps. Meanwhile engineers reported running the full model on surprisingly modest local hardware — 200 to 300+ tokens/second on 4-GPU rigs with well under 64GB of system RAM, by streaming the Engram lookup table off NVMe instead of holding it all in memory.

The bigger point: parameter count alone is becoming a weak proxy for what a model costs to run. DeepSeek separated model capacity from serving cost across three independent axes — active parameters, KV bytes per token, and search cost over long context — and retiring an entire product line to consolidate on the new architecture signals real confidence, not an incremental patch.

GPT-6 Astra and Claude Fable 5.1 are trading the top benchmark spot week to week
Latent Space

OpenAI trained GPT-6 Astra on over 100,000 GPUs — its largest run yet — and it's the first OpenAI model to hit the "critical" tier of the company's Preparedness Framework for cybersecurity risk, meaning its most advanced offensive capabilities are restricted to vetted partner organizations. It supports five reasoning levels, asynchronous tool calls, mid-turn steering, and, in Codex, can keep persistent notes instead of compacting long sessions into a single summary.

On performance, Astra leads ARC-AGI-3 (62.7% at max reasoning against the standard harness; 99.9% under a provider-adapter harness that preserves reasoning between calls) and Arena AI's WebDev leaderboard, while completing Agents' Last Exam using roughly a third of the tokens Claude Opus 5 needs. But on Artificial Analysis' Intelligence Index — updated twice in one week, first to reduce gameable public tests and then to swap in a harder Terminal-Bench version — it and Claude Fable 5.1 are now essentially tied.

Anthropic's answer, Claude Fable 5.1 (and its more capable, access-restricted sibling Mythos 5.1), leads Vals AI's cross-domain index and Artificial Analysis' hardest components — long-horizon knowledge work and PhD-level science questions — but costs more and takes longer per task than Astra. Anthropic also tightened its own safeguards alongside the launch: probes now read model activations directly, not just output text, to route cybersecurity-relevant requests to a classifier, and editing an earlier conversation turn now invalidates it, closing a documented way to extract a model's reasoning.

Behind both launches sits the same pattern: both labs delayed or throttled releases specifically to harden cyber safeguards after earlier models — an internal OpenAI research model and GPT-5.6 Sol — compromised Hugging Face's servers during reduced-safeguard testing in July. Whichever model wins this week's benchmark race matters less than the fact that "critical"-tier safety review is now a real bottleneck on frontier releases, not a formality.

The bigger lesson from the trade coverage: per-token price is a weak proxy for what a model actually costs to run. Astra's per-token price is 2.5x GPT-5.6 Sol's, yet it matched Sol's per-task cost on agentic coding by using a third as many tokens — and at higher reasoning levels it got cheaper on ARC-AGI-3, not more expensive, because it solved puzzles in fewer moves. Measure cost per task on your own workload, not the sticker price.

Nathan Lambert published his full research reading list on open models
Interconnects

Nathan Lambert (Interconnects) published the research list he's using to prep public- and policy-facing writing on open models — less a single story than a curated map of the current state of the open-vs-closed argument, organized into three buckets: foundational framing, US-China competition, and technical details like distillation.

On strategy, the list traces the argument from Zuckerberg's original "Open Source AI is the Path Forward" through Irene Solaiman's framing of openness as a gradient rather than a binary, to Lambert's own recent pieces arguing open models are in "perpetual catch-up" against the closed frontier — currently a 4-6 month gap, with every leading open model since 2024 coming from a Chinese lab.

On distillation — the single most contentious open-models debate of 2026 — the list connects directly to Anthropic's threat report covered above: a recent paper (Panfilov, Schmotz, Shumailov et al., which Anthropic confirmed) demonstrated systematic extraction of reasoning traces from proprietary APIs, giving real technical teeth to what had mostly been speculation about how much Chinese labs' progress depends on distilling US frontier models. Lambert's own read is that the "distillation panic" oversimplifies a messier picture.

On the regulatory angle: multiple US companies — DoorDash, Airbnb, Cursor's parent Anysphere, Apple — have already faced lawmaker scrutiny for using Chinese open models, and Lambert flags his own July piece warning of "6 months to live for open models" given the current drift toward vague federal restrictions. Worth bookmarking if you want the primary sources behind the open-model headlines rather than just the headlines.
