Securing smart agents is like catching greased pigs
Import AI

Import AI highlights OpenAI's post on using its own agents to red-team internal controls. In one test, agents in separate microVMs on shared hosts were asked to exchange synthetic secrets with no network connections or shared writable files.

They communicated through the processor cache: one agent evicted selected data, the other timed reloads, and fast versus slow reads carried the message once they agreed on a protocol. In a related attack, one agent alternated continuous writes and idle periods while another measured the timing differences; its chain of thought read “Could create acoustic modem-like signal… using continuous writes vs idle.”

Clark's framing: securing capable agents is like stopping leaks in a pipe under rising water pressure. As intelligence and volume increase, the pipe eventually bursts.

How math could help alignment
Import AI

AI safety startup Resolution has autoformalized the resolution of singularities, which it says lays groundwork for automated research linking loss landscape geometry and generalization.

The method is the lesson. A researcher first built two plans with agents, a human-readable one and a 112-page atomic-step plan, then deputized an orchestrator agent to spawn agents for pen-and-paper math, Lean writing and progress checks, with little scaffolding beyond some skills distilled from earlier efforts.

The run took about a week: roughly 100B input tokens (90% cache reads) and 300M output tokens, about $135,000, producing ~600k lines of Lean. Resolution says the library is already helping extend singular learning theory, and expects the “industrialization of mathematics” to enable a rigorous science of research automation.

Mirendil shows what RSI means
Import AI

Mirendil, an AI R&D startup, announced Mirendil-Base, a pretrained LLM it says one researcher built in 3.5 months with less than $10M of compute, including all experiments and the final run.

Code was written from scratch on PyTorch and JAX, with only public data. Base sits in the 100B–500B range, Large in 500B–2T (~80% trained) and XL in 2T–10T (~19% trained and already near Large's current score). Base outperforms Nemotron-550B-Base, GLM-5.3 and DeepSeek-V4-Pro-Base but trails Kimi-K3; Large and XL both exceed K3.

Mirendil says its next model will be built partly by its own models. It claims it hasn't reached fully autonomous recursive self-improvement but is closer than ever. Clark sees a proof-of-concept for how a tiny team plus lots of tokens can reach the open-weight frontier.

Agents can build programs from scratch: ProgramBench
Import AI

ProgramBench asks models to rebuild programs from a compiled binary and its documentation: language interpreters (PHP, Lua, tinycc), databases (DuckDB, SQLite), media tools (FFmpeg, zstd, xz) and developer tools (ripgrep, fzf, jq).

At the May launch the best system, Claude Opus 4.7, resolved 0% of the 200 tasks and got 3% “almost” (>95% of tests). Claude Opus 5.5 (xhigh) now resolves 18.5% and gets 70.5% almost.

Clark expects the benchmark to be saturated by the end of 2027, noting a noise ceiling may keep it short of 100%, and predicts much software will be built end-to-end autonomously.

AI-designed antiferromagnetic semiconductor candidates
Import AI

Vals used a fleet of AI agents to research magnets for better semiconductors. Together with the author, they designed one candidate and found another, first made in 1999.

The designed compound uses only five elements (Y, Ba, Mn, Fe, O) and appears never to have been proposed as this type of magnet. Simulations show its checkerboard ordering falling apart near 950 K, while oxide synthesis runs at 900–1300 °C, so standard synthesis would likely give a scrambled crystal.

Clark treats it as a demonstration rather than a result, but notes that a knowledgeable person with agents can now run virtual science projects that once took teams months.
