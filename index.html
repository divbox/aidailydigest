Artificial Analysis v5 and the benchmarker's dilemma
Interconnects

Nathan Lambert uses Artificial Analysis's Intelligence Index v5 as a prompt for a broader point: hill-climbing is easy, knowing what to hill-climb is hard. Every benchmark builder must reduce a jagged, fast-moving capability surface to one number, and every aggregation method carries trade-offs. With a trillion-dollar market partly steered by these rankings, the stakes for independent evaluators are high.

v5 is weighted in four equal quarters: Knowledge Work (AA-Briefcase 15%, AutomationBench-AA 5%, GDP.pdf 5%), Coding (FrontierCode 2.0 15%, Terminal-Bench 4.0 10%), Science (Terminal-Bench-Science 15%, CritPt v2 hard subset 10%), and General (AA-Omniscience v1.1 15%, HLE-Diamond 10%). The ranking looks reasonable at a glance, though it shows strain, such as GPT 6.1 Terra ranking above Claude Fable 5.1. Lambert reads the index as tuned toward a general agentic collaborator at the absolute frontier rather than repetitive agentic tasks.

The visible consequence is a wider open vs. closed gap: eight closed models beat the best open-weight entry, Kimi K3.1 Pro, with the largest deficits on the science evals. Lambert says the previous index flattered open models, and that open labs' value proposition was never to match the frontier but to commoditize high-demand inference and let companies own and specialize their intelligence. He expects open labs to make big steps again in 2-3 months (GLM 6, Kimi 3.5) and notes that the best open models benchmax a little, are somewhat narrower, and may lack the sophisticated RL stack needed to keep up across domains.

On AA's shift to private benchmarks, he calls it directionally defensible as an anti-benchmaxxing measure but costly: you now trust the builder completely and lose community bug-finding. He also notes Anthropic and OpenAI stopped caring about such indices long ago, while small and mid-sized labs depend on them for branding. His proposed fix is more indices with different biases (coding-only, like CursorBench; Vals AI; Intelligence.ai), and he is candid about the trust problem: AA and Arena are paid by labs, while he trusts non-profits, academics, and bodies like CAISI most.
